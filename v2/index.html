Ask a chatbot what amplitude modulation is, and it can answer in many ways: a formal definition, a worked example, a string of leading questions, an analogy, a short cheat-sheet, or numbered steps with encouragement. Which answer is best depends on who is asking. A first-year student who missed the lecture may need every step named in plain words. Someone revising for an exam may want one line they can quote.

A system that serves many people cannot ask each of them to fill in a questionnaire first. What it can do is show two answers and see which one the person picks. Each new user is a stranger who stays for a few questions, so the system has to learn quickly, and the main way to learn quickly is to reuse what earlier users revealed.

We built TutorDuel, a benchmark for this problem, around a tutoring case study. Simulated students with hidden preferences arrive one after another, each asks 24 questions, and a policy must choose which two of six tutoring styles to show every time. The policy is charged for both answers it shows, and the only feedback is the student's choice.

We also propose CODuel, a first baseline that learns from completed sessions which kinds of students recur. On students and topics never used during development, it removes 44 to 48 percent of the extra regret that random choices incur, a little over half of what a per-student oracle fitted in hindsight achieves.

The problem

Each round has the same shape. A student asks a question, the system picks two of the six tutoring styles, both answers are generated, and the student chooses one. The figure below walks through a round.

Every student comes once. A student's preferences stay fixed for their 24 questions, and then they leave. Students are different, but not entirely: some kinds of students recur, and that shared structure is what a system can learn across students.

Styles are described before anything is generated. Each style is a system prompt, and a cheap rater model scores how well each style suits a question from the question text alone. A policy can therefore rank the six styles without generating any answers. Only the two it shows are produced.

Both answers count. We charge the policy for each displayed answer by how far it falls short of the student's best style on that question. Even a policy that knows the student perfectly pays something, because it must show a second-best answer next to the best one. We subtract that unavoidable part and call the rest extra regret.

The score is the share of extra regret removed. Picking pairs at random scores 0. Showing the student's two best styles on every question would score 100, which no policy is expected to reach. As a realistic ceiling, an oracle that knows each student's preference weights in hindsight scores 81 to 85.

Exact definitions
rt = 2·Ui(p*, qt) − Ui(pt,1, qt) − Ui(pt,2, qt)
RT = Σt rt    excess regret = RT − GT
benchmark score = 100 · (RTrand − RT) / (RTrand − GT)

Ui(p, q) = θi⊤φ(p, q) is student i's utility for persona p on question q, and the student's choice follows a Bradley–Terry–Luce model on the two displayed answers. GT is the distinct-pair floor: the regret of always showing the best and second-best persona. In the paper's terms the problem is a multi-user contextual dueling bandit with strong regret.

What is in the benchmark

Six tutoring styles. Each is one system prompt. Open a style to read its prompt.

Questions. Each session has 24 questions across four topics, written by an LLM. Every question ends with a short first-person cue, such as “I need a line I can quote in an exam,” so the best style can change from one question to the next.

Students. Each simulated student is a written profile. Sixteen were written by hand, eight for development and eight held out. Two larger groups of 350 students each are generated from a template of seven traits, one with distinct trait combinations and one with added quirks, such as disliking numbered lists.

The judge. An LLM plays each student and picks between two answers. We asked it about every pair of styles in both display orders, many times over, and stored the probabilities: 2.4 million prompts in total. Benchmark runs replay these stored probabilities, so evaluating a policy needs no GPU and no API key.

Checks. Two authors labeled 200 choices by hand. The judge agreed with each of them about as often as they agreed with each other. A second, different judge agreed with the first on 81 percent of the comparisons where neither was indifferent, and CODuel ranked first under both.

Results

Every method starts with no data and serves the students in order. A method that treats each student alone does not get better over time. CODuel also treats each student alone at first, and switches on sharing once it has seen at least eight students and sharing predicts their choices better. From then on, it gets better with every student.

In the last quarter of the students, CODuel's extra regret is 31, 27 and 30 percent lower than that of the same learner without sharing, on the three evaluation groups.

Sharing is easy to get wrong. On the eight held-out hand-written profiles, every other method that shares information across students does worse than learning each student alone. On the two larger template groups the same methods do better than learning alone, but CODuel still leads.

Most of the remaining gap is identifying the student. Showing each student's single best pair of styles throughout, chosen in hindsight, already scores 66 to 76. The oracle, which can also change the pair from one question to the next, scores 81 to 85.

Sharing switches off when it should. When we scramble each student's choices so that students have nothing in common, CODuel stays within one percent of learning alone.

How CODuel works

CODuel keeps a belief about who the current student is, and builds that belief from the students it has already served.

It learns which kinds of students recur. After each session, it updates a small set of student types, each a cluster of similar preferences. It always keeps room for a student unlike any seen so far.

It narrows down each new student. A new student starts as a mix of all known types. Each choice shifts the mix toward the types that explain the choices so far.

It asks informative questions early. When the types disagree about which pair to show, CODuel may show a pair whose outcome tells them apart. This costs a little now and saves more over the rest of the session.

It corrects the rater. The rater that scores styles for each question is good at recognizing some needs and worse at others. CODuel learns a correction from the choices of past students.

It shares only when sharing helps. A copy that learns each student alone runs alongside. Both predict every choice before it is revealed, and CODuel uses what it learned from other students only while that prediction is better.

All settings were fixed on the development students. The paper gives the full procedure and the sensitivity to each setting.

The held-out groups need --allow-test, a guard against looking at them by accident during development. CODuel takes one to two minutes per run on one CPU core.

Limitations

All preferences are simulated. The same LLM judge produces the choices and the hidden preferences they are scored against, and the only human labels are the authors' 200 choices. We have not measured whether real students are more satisfied or learn more, and the style a student prefers need not be the one that helps them learn best.

CODuel assumes that the population of students does not change over time and that each student's preferences stay fixed within a session. We give no theoretical guarantee. Closing the gap to the oracle and testing with real users are the next steps.
