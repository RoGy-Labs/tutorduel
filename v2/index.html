Ask a chatbot what amplitude modulation is and it can answer in several ways: a formal definition, a worked example, a series of leading questions, an analogy, a short list of bullet points, or numbered steps with encouragement. The best answer depends on who asks. A first-year student who missed the lecture may need each step named in plain words, while a student revising for an exam may want one line to quote.

A system that serves many people cannot ask each of them to fill in a questionnaire first. It can show two answers and record which one the person picks. Each new user stays for only a few questions, so the system has to reuse what earlier users revealed.

TutorDuel is a benchmark for this problem, built around tutoring. Simulated students with hidden preferences arrive one after another, and each asks 24 questions. For every question, a policy picks two of six tutor personas to answer, pays for both answers, and observes only the student's choice.

CODuel is our baseline for it. It learns from completed sessions which kinds of students recur. On students and topics never used during development, it removes 44 to 48% of the excess regret of random pairs, a little over half of what a per-student oracle fitted in hindsight removes.

The problem

Every round follows the same steps (Figure 1). A student asks a question, the policy picks two of the six personas, both answers are generated, and the student chooses one.

Each student comes once, keeps the same preferences for 24 questions, and leaves. Students differ, yet some kinds of students recur, and a policy can learn that structure from one student to the next.

A persona is a system prompt that sets a teaching style. Before any answer is generated, a cheap rater model scores how well each persona suits the question from the question text alone. A policy can therefore rank the six personas first and generate only the two answers it shows.

The policy pays for both answers it shows. For each one, it pays the gap between the student's best persona and the persona shown. A policy that knew the student perfectly would still pay for showing the second-best answer next to the best one, so we subtract this unavoidable cost and call the remainder the excess regret.

The benchmark score is the percentage of random pairs' excess regret that a policy removes. Random pairs score 0, and showing each student's two best personas on every question would score 100. No policy is expected to reach 100. An oracle that knows each student's preference weights in hindsight scores 81 to 85.

Exact definitions
rt = 2·Ui(p*, qt) − Ui(pt,1, qt) − Ui(pt,2, qt)
RT = Σt rt    excess regret = RT − GT
benchmark score = 100 · (RTrand − RT) / (RTrand − GT)

Ui(p, q) = θi⊤φ(p, q) is student i's utility for persona p on question q, and the student's choice follows a Bradley–Terry–Luce model on the two displayed answers. GT is the distinct-pair floor, the regret of always showing the best and second-best personas. In the paper's terms, the problem is a multi-user contextual dueling bandit with strong regret.

What is in the benchmark

Each of the six personas is one system prompt. Open a persona to read its prompt.

A session has 24 questions on four topics, written by an LLM. Each question ends with a short first-person cue, such as "I need a line I can quote in an exam", so the best persona can change from one question to the next.

Each simulated student has a written profile. We wrote sixteen profiles by hand (eight for development, eight held out) and generated two groups of 350 students from a template of seven traits. One group uses distinct trait combinations; the other adds quirks, such as a dislike of numbered lists.

An LLM judge plays each student and picks between two answers. We asked it about every pair of personas, in both display orders and several times each, and stored the probabilities, 2.4 million prompts in total. Benchmark runs replay the stored probabilities, so evaluating a policy needs no GPU and no API key.

To check the judge, two authors labeled 200 choices by hand. The judge agreed with each author about as often as the two authors agreed with each other. A second judge, built on a different model, agreed with the first on 81% of the comparisons where neither was indifferent, and CODuel ranked first under both.

Results

Every method starts with no data and serves the students in order (Figure 2). A method that learns each student alone stays flat. CODuel also learns each student alone at first. Once it has seen at least eight students and the shared model predicts their choices better, it switches sharing on and improves with each new student.

Over the last quarter of the students, CODuel's excess regret is 31%, 27% and 30% lower than that of the same learner without sharing on the three evaluation groups.

On the eight held-out hand-written profiles, every other method that shares information across students does worse than learning each student alone. On the two template groups, those methods do better than learning alone, and CODuel stays ahead of them.

Most of the remaining gap comes from identifying the student. The single pair that works best for all students scores 5 to 22 (the best fixed pair in the table below). Each student's own best pair, chosen in hindsight, scores 66 to 76, and the oracle, which can also change the pair from one question to the next, scores 81 to 85.

When we scramble each student's choices so that students share nothing, CODuel stays within 1% of learning alone, because its check turns sharing off.

How CODuel works

CODuel keeps a belief about who the current student is and builds it from the students it has already served. After each session, it updates a small set of student types, each a cluster of similar preferences, and keeps some weight for a student unlike any seen so far. A new student starts as a mix of all known types, and each choice moves the mix toward the types that explain the choices so far.

When the types disagree about which pair to show, CODuel may show a pair whose outcome tells them apart. It pays a little on that question and saves more over the rest of the session.

The rater's scores are more accurate for some needs than for others, so CODuel also learns a correction to them from past students' choices.

A second learner, which treats each student alone, runs alongside. Both predict every choice before it is revealed, and CODuel uses the shared model only while it predicts better.

All settings were fixed on the development students. The paper gives the full procedure and the sensitivity to each setting.

The held-out groups require --allow-test, which guards against looking at them by accident during development. CODuel takes one to two minutes per run on one CPU core.

Limitations

All preferences are simulated. The same LLM judge produces the choices and the utilities they are scored against, and the only human labels are the 200 choices two authors made. We have not measured whether real students are more satisfied or learn more, and the persona a student prefers may not be the one that teaches them best.

CODuel assumes that the student population does not change over time and that each student's preferences stay fixed during a session. We give no theoretical guarantee.
